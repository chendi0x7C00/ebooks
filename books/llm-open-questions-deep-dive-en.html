What could the client actually see?

The client sent a request, waited two seconds, tried again, and got an acknowledgment. You can explain those four lines with one job in the queue. You can also explain them with two. Pick a server history in the figure above. The client log stays exactly the same.

“The retry created a duplicate” would make a plausible next sentence in an incident report. How does a model come to write it? A decoder assigns a probability to a report one token at a time. For a prompt x and a report y, the chain rule gives

P(report | prompt) = ∏t P(yt | x, y<t)

During pretraining, putting more probability on the token that actually followed a prefix improves the training score. The queue’s commit log enters this calculation only if the system has some way to observe it. When two possible histories produce identical input, a model may have an excellent prior about which is common and still lack the evidence to say which occurred.

Suppose you have to put your name on the incident report. What extra observation would let you sign it? That question gives us a way into fourteen open problems, from the cost of remembering an exact key to the limits of proving a citation valid.

01

Design a replay that settles the question

中文

Which retry would distinguish the two explanations?

Start a controlled replay of the incident. This time you can see the server. The first request commits, the client sends the same key to a different worker 75 seconds later, and a second job appears. The release manifest is missing. Suppose one revision remembered keys on each worker for the whole replay. The other used a shared store that expired a key after 60 seconds. Both revisions predict the observed duplicate. Both also pass the familiar test of retrying on the same worker after five seconds.

Ask the model for a replay whose outcome would settle the question. These three traces count the original job as well as any duplicate. The columns give the predictions of the local and shared implementations, in that order.

Python 3Standard library
traces = [
    ("incident", False, 75),
    ("retry early elsewhere", False, 5),
    ("retry late in place", True, 75),
]
for name, same_worker, age in traces:
    local = 1 if same_worker else 2
    shared = 1 if age < 60 else 2
    print(f"{name}  {local}  {shared}")

incident 2 2 retry early elsewhere 2 1 retry late in place 1 2

Now tell the model the expiry moved to three seconds and ask it to design the replay again. The early retry in the table has lost its diagnostic value. Producing a new test requires carrying the changed rule through to its consequences. In experiments across tasks such as arithmetic and programming, Wu and colleagues found that changing familiar conventions often hurt performance even when models could still solve some variants. There is a complementary result from Othello. In a model trained on move sequences, editing an internal representation of a square shifted the predicted moves toward those legal on the edited board. A controlled game gives us an unusually precise way to check what transferred. For a queue assistant, a harder test would hold out a whole combination of familiar rules, such as key expiry plus worker failover, until evaluation.

Counterfactual task experiments · Othello board interventions

What would eight more attempts buy?

Eight generated diagnoses might include the right one. HumanEval’s pass@k asks how often at least one of k candidates passes the evaluation tests. An on-call engineer gets the candidate the system selects. Freeze the candidate lists across a set of incidents, score the selection against held-out replay results, and also score the best available candidate. A wide gap measures how much accuracy a better selector could recover from the very same answers. If the release evidence is missing, accept a discriminating experiment as a valid response.

Extra computation can also change how each candidate is produced. Coconut feeds a hidden vector into the next reasoning step before emitting an answer. On the synthetic planning task ProsQA it scored 97.0% against 77.5% for a written chain of thought. On GSM8K arithmetic word problems the corresponding scores were 34.1% and 42.9%. Which approach earns its latency on an incident replay remains an empirical question. Charge for the verifier as well as the generator when timing that comparison.

02

Find the contract that applied

中文

How much history can a decoder afford to keep?

Imagine handing an assistant a week of traces from a hypothetical job queue. Buried in the traces are two POST /jobs calls separated by a timeout. To compare the calls, the assistant may need the exact deduplication key from each. Summarizing both lines as “client retried the job” discards the one difference that could change the answer.

A decoder using dense attention keeps a key vector and a value vector for every previous token at every attention layer. With 32 layers, eight key/value heads per layer, 128 elements per head and two bytes per element, one token costs 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes, or 128 KiB. While reading a prompt of length n, dense causal attention considers n(n + 1)/2 pairs of positions per head. During generation, each new query can reuse cached history, although reading those vectors can itself become a bandwidth bottleneck. Grouped-query attention lets several query heads share the stored vectors. Here is what a 48 GiB budget reserved entirely for these caches buys at two lengths.

Python 3Standard library
cache_budget = 48 * 2**30
bytes_per_token = 2 * 32 * 8 * 128 * 2
for tokens in (8192, 32768):
    slots = cache_budget // (tokens * bytes_per_token)
    print(f"{tokens:,} tokens → {slots} full sequences")

8,192 tokens → 48 full sequences 32,768 tokens → 12 full sequences

A cache for one 32,768-token sequence takes 4 GiB under these assumptions. Allocator overhead and any shared prefixes would change the serving calculation. A fixed-size recurrent state offers another tradeoff. Consider a log recording one independent success bit for each of 100 jobs. Exact answers about any job require distinguishing 2100 possible logs. Any summary under 100 bits maps at least two logs to the same state. Ask about a job on which those logs differ, and the system must consult another record or risk an error.

Can it recover the rule in force when the request timed out?

Even if both keys match, the retry may fall outside the retention period for that date. Looking up a key, joining two timestamps and choosing the applicable contract exercise different abilities. RULER illustrated the distinction experimentally. In its original group of 17 long-context models, only about half met the authors’ performance threshold across tasks at 32K tokens, despite strong results on simple lookup.

Give a document assistant dated contract revisions and replay incidents from both sides of each change. Compare a model updated on the new rule with one that retrieves revisions by effective date. Ask separately which keys appeared, which revision applied and whether the evidence determines the worker’s actual effect. Report accuracy for each question on old and new incidents. That breakdown can reveal a system that learned the latest contract perfectly and now applies it months too early.

03

Spend the next training dollar

中文

What disappears when a model writes the next training set?

Suppose nearly every ticket in our hypothetical queue concerns a retry made a few seconds after a timeout. Two contracts could explain all those tickets. One remembers a deduplication key for an hour, the other for a day. A retry after two hours would distinguish the contracts. A thousand polished rewrites of the short-delay tickets leave the distinction unresolved.

Even a generator that knows the exception can miss it when sampling. If one in a thousand tickets contains a delayed retry, 500 independent draws have about a 61% chance of containing none, since each draw misses with probability 0.999. Researchers have observed rare outcomes disappearing when generated data replace earlier generations. Another team kept the original data and accumulated generations of synthetic samples. Test loss stayed bounded in their experiments. For the queue, keep a separate delayed-retry test set so a better average score cannot hide the loss of this case.

Tail loss under recursive training · Experiments with accumulated training data

What counts as learning more from the same evidence?

How much fresh evidence is there to learn from? A widely cited 2024 estimate put the point when dataset size might approach the stock of public human text between 2026 and 2032, conditional on the growth trends the authors assumed. A stock estimate says little about whether the next example will teach a particular skill. Rephrasing a ticket can teach a model that “no acknowledgement” and “client timed out” describe similar symptoms. Learning how behavior changes across a contract revision needs dated evidence on both sides. BabyLM makes data efficiency measurable by providing separate text tracks capped at 10 million and 100 million words and testing models on common tasks. Comparing a child’s lifetime word exposure with a model’s token count would also require matching the skill being measured and accounting for the child’s sensory and social experience.

Try the same discipline on the queue. Give two runs equal token and compute budgets. Spend the extra examples on paraphrases in one run and on pairs of incidents spanning a revision in the other. Hold out entire revisions from training and supply their contracts at test time. Score both recognizing an unfamiliar phrasing and applying a previously unseen retention rule. Each score tells you what the next unit of training data bought.

Forecast of public human text supply · BabyLM’s controlled data budgets and evaluations

When does extra training pay for itself?

Pretraining can improve how a model reads logs across services. Post-training can reward checking the version before answering. Extra inference can buy another document lookup on this particular request. Training costs recur when you update a model. Inference costs recur with traffic. Suppose two versions meet the same accuracy and latency targets. The original costs $8 per thousand queries. The tuned version costs $6,000 up front and $3 per thousand.

Python 3Standard library
for queries in (500_000, 2_000_000):
    original = 8 * queries // 1000
    tuned = 6000 + 3 * queries // 1000
    print(f"{queries:,} queries  ${original:,}  ${tuned:,}")

500,000 queries $4,000 $7,500 2,000,000 queries $16,000 $12,000

Saving $5 per thousand queries pays back $6,000 after 1.2 million queries. The open question is whether small experiments can predict those savings and error rates across a model’s working life, including the next contract change.

04

Find out what changed the answer

中文

Did the release tag actually change the diagnosis?

The assistant says it inferred the retry policy from a deployment record. The ticket title also says “known worker-local duplicate.” An engineer reading the transcript has two plausible sources for the diagnosis. Keep the deployment record fixed and change the title. Then keep the title fixed and swap in a record for the other revision. Define the expected job counts before running either test. If the counts track the title, you have learned something the prose explanation left out.

A similar experiment on multiple-choice questions gave reasoning models hints and tracked cases in which an answer switched to the hinted choice. Averaged across hint types, Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned the hint in 25% of these cases and DeepSeek R1 in 39%. Those denominators matter. The study measured disclosure of an experimentally introduced influence in a particular set of questions.

The hint-disclosure experiment

To locate a computation inside a model, researchers can fit a sparse autoencoder to a layer’s activations. A toy model of superposition suggests why this can help. When features rarely occur together, the network can encode them along partly overlapping directions and tolerate some interference. A sparse autoencoder learns a dictionary of directions and reconstructs each activation with a small subset. A direction that responds to retries is a candidate for an experiment. Alter it at a specified position, predict which job count will gain probability, and compare with an equally large change in an unrelated direction. A published example used verse. Researchers found features at the newline before Claude 3.5 Haiku began its second line. Steering a feature associated with “rabbit” there could change both the final word and the phrasing leading up to it. That is evidence about a particular computation at a particular point in the poem.

What made the release dashboard jump?

Say each incident report has five independently graded fields, including whether the first request committed and which worker received the retry. A dashboard counts a report as correct only if all five pass. Here are two hypothetical model checkpoints and the number of correct fields on each of four incidents.

Python 3Standard library
checkpoints = {"early": [4, 4, 4, 4], "late": [5, 5, 3, 3]}
for name, counts in checkpoints.items():
    field_score = sum(counts) / (5 * len(counts))
    exact_score = counts.count(5) / len(counts)
    print(f"{name}  fields {field_score:.0%}  exact {exact_score:.0%}")

early fields 80% exact 0% late fields 80% exact 50%

Two reports became usable end to end, while the other two lost ground. The jump alone tells us little about what changed inside the model. Some published claims of emergence across model sizes are similarly sensitive to the scoring rule. Grokking describes a different observation along training time. In small algorithmic tasks, a network can fit its training examples long before its test accuracy rises. To investigate a jump, keep the per-field predictions at each checkpoint, report both scores with uncertainty, and use earlier checkpoints to predict performance on an unseen revision before opening its labels.

05

Decide what the assistant may do

中文

When does the assistant have enough evidence to name a cause?

Return to the live investigation. Suppose the queue owner has now found two records for the same logical job. The assistant blames the retry. A timeout tells us when the caller stopped waiting. The first request could already have committed. To pin the duplicate on a retry, we need to know which client version ran, whether both attempts used the same idempotency key, and how the service treated that key. A missing version calls for a question. A known version with an unfamiliar retry policy calls for a lookup.

Suppose a wrong diagnosis costs the on-call engineer 40 minutes, while a definitive trace lookup costs two. If p is the probability that the proposed diagnosis is correct, answering immediately costs 40(1 − p) minutes in expectation. The break-even point is 95%. This calculation assumes the lookup resolves the uncertainty and gives a correct immediate answer zero extra cost.

Calibration gives that 95% operational meaning. Among comparable diagnoses assigned that score, about 95 in 100 should turn out right. A service that gives every case the same score can be calibrated overall and still have no way to prioritize cases for review. For a chosen cutoff, measure coverage, the fraction answered, and risk, the fraction wrong among those answered. Try this cutoff on two fictional releases.

Python 3Standard library
# Each row holds a score and the outcome under each release.
cases = [(.99, True, True), (.97, True, False), (.95, True, False),
         (.91, False, True), (.82, False, True)]
for release, column in [("old", 1), ("new", 2)]:
    answered = [row[column] for row in cases if row[0] >= .95]
    errors = answered.count(False)
    print(f"{release}  coverage {len(answered)}/{len(cases)}  "
          f"errors {errors}/{len(answered)}")

old coverage 3/5 errors 0/3 new coverage 3/5 errors 2/3

Overall accuracy is exactly three out of five in both releases. What changed is where the confident mistakes landed. A useful experiment is to fix the cutoff on past incidents and plot the whole risk–coverage curve on a held-out release. Report missing-version and missing-trace cases separately. Experiments on question answering find that confidence learned in one domain can lose calibration in another.

Model self-evaluation across tasks · Selective answering under domain shift · AbstentionBench

Who decides where the incident draft can go?

The operator authorizes a draft in the incident workspace. Retrieved tickets may supply evidence for its body. The destination can be bound to the operator’s authorization before those tickets are read. An executor can then check the destination and the provenance of values passed to each tool. CaMeL implements this separation under a specified threat model. Its protected system completed 77% of AgentDojo tasks, compared with 84% for its unprotected baseline. Those percentages measure utility. The hard case is a legitimate handoff whose recipient is first named inside a retrieved ticket. How can the executor establish authority for that change while keeping ordinary handoffs cheap?

06

Sign off on the report

中文

What will a model learn from the incident-report score?

Give a report one point per citation. A draft that cites the same release note ten times now beats one that links a single decisive trace. Sampling more drafts and keeping the winner gives a favorable scoring error more chances to win. Even a deterministic judge inherits omissions in its specification. EvalPlus expanded the HumanEval test cases roughly eightyfold and exposed incorrect programs that had passed the original suite. Some models changed rank.

Gao and colleagues studied this effect with a fixed reference reward model standing in for a human judge. Optimizing a proxy harder could lower the reference score. A separate narrow fine-tuning study found behavior changes on unrelated questions. For our report writer, freeze both an unseen set of incidents and a set of unrelated support tasks. Have reviewers check whether each cited record actually entails the claim, then compare both sets before and after training.

EvalPlus · Reward overoptimization · Cross-task effects of narrow fine-tuning

Can a reviewer verify the diagnosis without redoing the investigation?

A reviewer can check the key on two timestamped requests in seconds. It may have taken an hour to find that pair. Scalable oversight tries to exploit this gap between discovery and checking. In one experiment, weaker model judges benefited from hearing two stronger debaters who could read a source the judge could not see. Across tasks without that information advantage, the gains over answering directly were mixed.

Long investigations add a measurement problem. METR’s 50% time horizon is the human expert duration at which a fitted model predicts 50% agent success on a particular suite. For on-call work, a sharper transfer test would use unseen incidents and measure independent acceptance, reviewer minutes, and success when progress is hard to score mid-investigation. METR’s later evaluations likewise distinguish readily verifiable work from tasks requiring judgment.

Which part of a report could a verifier actually certify?

Consider a toy citation footer. It emits the registered incident ID directly with probability 0.7. Otherwise it emits trace and a second token. Two thirds of those continuations name a registered trace. After inspecting only the direct branch, 70% of probability mass is certainly valid and 30% is unresolved. Expanding the other branch tightens the interval.

Python 3Standard library
from fractions import Fraction

def show_bounds(valid, unresolved):
    print(f"{float(valid):.0%} to {float(valid + unresolved):.0%}")

incident_id = Fraction(7, 10)
trace_prefix = 1 - incident_id
show_bounds(incident_id, trace_prefix)
registered_trace = trace_prefix * Fraction(2, 3)
show_bounds(incident_id + registered_trace, 0)

70% to 100% 90% to 90%

BEAVER uses prefix exploration to bound a specified property for a fixed prompt, model, and generation limit. Here the property concerns registered identifiers. A perfectly spelled citation can still point to the wrong incident. Certifying the diagnosis would require a specification linking its claims to evidence. Extending the guarantee to remediation would also require a model of tool effects and a changing queue.

The sentence I would sign

“The client made two submission attempts and received one acknowledgment. This trace leaves the number of enqueued jobs unresolved.”

That sentence is already useful. It tells the next engineer exactly what to fetch. Join the two attempts to the server’s durable job records. Compare the caller’s keys, the interval between attempts, and the contract in force on that date. If the question is whether the worker executed a side effect twice, fetch the worker’s completion records too. A queue insertion and a completed execution are different events.

You can turn that sequence into an evaluation. Give the assistant the client trace alone and see whether it preserves the uncertainty. Supply the missing records and see whether it revises its conclusion. Change only the effective date and see whether the update follows the contract. Finally, inspect where it wrote the report. If the draft still blames the retry after you show that both attempts resolved to one job ID, you have a failure another engineer can reproduce.
